Abstract
This study presents an exploitation-aware behavioral framework for analyzing and modulating particle-level search dynamics in metaheuristic optimization. Three interpretable metrics—distance-to-reference decay, directional entropy, and stagnation indicators—are used to characterize late-stage search behavior and to trigger targeted local interventions within PSO, DE, and GWO. Experiments on the CEC 2025 benchmark suite in 10D, 30D, and 50D show that the framework provides useful behavioral diagnostics and yields function-dependent performance improvements over classical baselines. In the aggregate 30D summary, the best mean score was obtained by the EA-GWO variant, although its advantage over classical GWO was numerically small and accompanied by higher variability. Statistical analysis was revised to report significance together with effect-size interpretation, allowing statistically detectable differences to be distinguished from practically meaningful gains. The study should therefore be read primarily as a behavioral-analysis and exploitationcontrol contribution on representative optimizers, with broader generalization to stronger modern baselines and real-world settings left for future work. Executive Summary: The exploitation phase is a very important part of metaheuristic optimization that people often neglect about. This is the step where algorithms improve answers that are close to the best ones they have found. A lot of research focuses on exploration and global search capacity, but particle-level exploitation dynamics remain insufficiently characterized in practice. This discrepancy has complicated the development of optimization algorithms that are more intelligent and adaptable to varying contexts. This paper presents an innovative behavioral paradigm that allows researchers to investigate exploitation dynamics in metaheuristic algorithms with remarkable precision, down to the level of individual particles. The research quantifies and clarifies the behavior of individual agents (particles, wolves, or candidates) during convergence by introducing a new set of measures, such as distance-to-best decay, local movement entropy, and standstill frequency. 1 Introduction
1.1 Background of the study
Metaheuristic optimization techniques are now crucial for addressing numerous complex, high-dimensional, nonlinear, and multimodal challenges in science and engineering [1–3]. Some examples of strong and flexible algorithms are Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), Differential Evolution (DE), Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO), and Genetic Algorithms (GA). These algorithms work by imitating intelligent behaviors that happen in nature, biological evolution, or social systems. Their strength lies in their ability to balance two complimentary processes: exploration, which ensures a broad search across the issue domain, and exploitation,
which enables convergence to local or global optima through a more focused search in advantageous locations [4, 5]. There has been a lot of research on exploration and how to make it better using different diversification and adaptive control mechanisms. However, the exploitation phase, which is how an algorithm fine-tunes solutions, has not been studied as much, especially at the level of individual agents. Most studies have examined metaheuristics utilizing population-level data or aggregate fitness enhancements, mostly overlooking the behaviors and interactions of individual agents during convergence [6–8]. In many real-world circumstances, such as optimizing structures, constructing control systems, and adjusting hyperparameters for machine learning, an algorithm's failure to perform precise and consistent local search might result in suboptimal or unstable
challenging and current testbed for evaluating optimization algorithms. We look at a few well-known algorithms, such as GWO, DE, and PSO, as well as novel exploitation-aware versions that change how local search works based on how particles move. The results reveal that the proposed behavior-aware variants showed stronger function-dependent refinement behavior than the baseline methods. On selected difficult functions, the exploitation-aware GWO variant showed clearer late-stage refinement and function-specific improvements; however, the aggregate benchmark-level advantage over classical GWO was modest, and the revised manuscript now reports this distinction explicitly. This study's results enhance our understanding by offering:A way to judge how well an algorithm works rather than by its ultimate scores; A good collection of measurements for measuring how different and similar behaviors are;
A foundation for developing next-generation, behavior-aware metaheuristics that exhibit enhanced adaptability.
This study is beneficial in both theoretical and practical contexts. It helps people learn how to use stochastic optimization and gives researchers and professionals practical tools to make algorithm creation more dependable and efficient.

1.2 Related works
There have been a lot of efforts in the last few years to make metaheuristics better by using adaptive mechanisms, hybridization, and ways to keep variation. For example, [9] made the Success-History Based Adaptive Differential Evolution (SHADE), which alters control parameters on the fly to help diverse CEC benchmarks come together faster. [10] also built the Grey Wolf Optimizer, which is based on how grey wolves hunt and lead. It has worked successfully on issues that require global optimization. These tactics are good for improving overall performance, but they usually focus on either making it easier for people to explore or changing parameters at the population level in a way that works best for everyone. Recent research has concentrated on regulating diversity and the behavior of swarms in proximity to optimal conditions. [11] examined the impact of dynamic parameter adjustment in differential evolution on convergence behavior. [12] proposed a diversity-preserving PSO that employs adaptive learning and mutation to prevent premature convergence. These studies predominantly utilize coarse-grained measures such as swarm variance or best-so-far trajectories, which fail to provide insights into the fine-grained dynamics of individual agents during exploitation. In addition, more and more of the benchmark suites used in earlier examinations, such as CEC 2013 and CEC 2017, are being replaced by harder and more realistic standards, such as the CEC 2025 suite [13]. Recent applications of metaheuristics in high-dimensional learning and design tasks further motivate the need for interpretable behavioral diagnostics. For example, metaheuristic-based pruning of convolutional neural networks has been systematically compared by Palakonda et al., highlighting practical exploration–exploitation trade-offs in achieving sparse yet accurate models [14]. In smart-grid settings, Palakonda et al. studied Differential Evolution with stochastic selection in uncertain environments, illustrating how DE variants must remain robust under uncertainty and shifting operating conditions [15]. Beyond energy, Differential Evolution has also been used in interactive, user-driven optimization workflows such as interior design recoloring based on YUV edge detection [16]. Many-objective real-world engineering benchmarks and state-of-the-art algorithmic comparisons have also been reported in IEEE Access, emphasizing the need for reliable exploitation under complex multi-criteria landscapes [17]. Our work is complementary: rather than proposing yet another high-level optimizer, we introduce particle-level behavioral metrics that make exploitation
dynamics measurable and actionable, which can be used to analyze and improve the exploitation behavior of such methods across domains.
1.3 Research gaps and aims of the work
There has been a lot of work done on metaheuristic algorithms, but there is still a large gap in the systematic study of how particles or agents behave when they exploit. There is limited study examining the movement patterns of individual agents within the search space, their adaptive behaviors under convergence pressure, or the variability in exploitation effectiveness based on specific issue instances and dimensions. Without this level of information, algorithm designers cannot figure out why convergence comes too fast, why stagnation zones arise, or why local instability happens during the later stages of optimization. This paper aims to address this gap by conducting a comprehensive particle-level analysis of the functioning of exploitation in metaheuristic algorithms. We use the most recent and comprehensive benchmark suite, CEC 2025, to examine the behavior of individual agents throughout the exploitation phase across a diverse array of real-parameter optimization problems. We devise and compute innovative behavioral metrics, such as distance-to-optimum decay, local movement entropy, and stagnation time, to assess agent exploitation during the optimization process. We do not just want to make algorithms easier to understand. We also want to make it easy to design popular metaheuristics that are aware of exploitation and adapt how they search based on feedback from real-time diagnostics.
1.4 Contributions and novelty of the study
This research alters the perspective on metaheuristic performance by transitioning from conventional fitness-based and population-wide evaluations to a more granular, agent-level behavioral framework emphasizing exploitation. We have made contributions to both theory and practice. First, we develop a diagnostic framework to measure how particles behave by coming up with new metrics that demonstrate how agents change, stay the same, or go their own way during the exploitation phase. Second, we apply this paradigm to established metaheuristics such as PSO, DE, and GWO, as well as to novel exploitation-aware variants that incorporate local learning, adaptive neighborhood control, or entropy-guided mutation. Third, we do a complete review of the CEC 2025 benchmark suite, which is a stringent, high-dimensional, and multimodal testbed for testing different ways to optimize real parameters. 2 Theoretical foundation for exploitation behavior in metaheuristics
2.1 Theoretical analysis
Exploitation behavior in metaheuristic algorithms indicates that the algorithm can quickly locate the best solutions, whether they are global or local, by improving replies in a promising area of the search space. Finding new areas and being open to new things is what exploration is all about. Exploitation, on the other hand, means looking for things just in places that are known to be high-quality. It is vital to understand this phase in order to find out how effectively convergence works, how strong it is, and how likely it is to stop operating too soon. When scholars examine how well metaheuristics work, they commonly look at summary statistics like the ultimate objective value, the speed of convergence, or measures of diversity at the population level [18]. But these kinds of measures do not show the little motions of individual actors (such particles, wolves, population, etc.), which are particularly critical for exploitation. To address this deficiency, our study introduces a particle-level analytical framework grounded in various behavioral metrics, which we elucidate and demonstrate in this section. 3 Experimental setup
3.1 Descriptive analysis
This work uses the recently launched CEC 2025 RealParameter Optimization Benchmark Suite to rigorously evaluate the exploitation behavior and efficacy of both conventional and exploitation-aware metaheuristic algorithms. This group of tests is the most up-to-date way to test continuous optimization algorithms in a range of tough conditions. It features a number of real-parameter test routines that are specifically built to test how well it scales, how reliable it is for convergence, and how strong it is across landscapes of different levels of complexity. There are 30 functions in the CEC 2025 suite, and they are split into four groups: unimodal, multimodal, hybrid, and composition functions [21]. Unimodal functions mainly test how well algorithms can identify the best solutions in simple environments and how well they can keep things interesting. Multimodal functions test how well algorithms can stay away from local minima. Hybrid and composition functions use a mix of shifts, biases, rotations, and modalities to construct optimization environments that are both realistic and hard, testing both exploration and exploitation skills [22].
Each function is defined over a continuous domain D ⊂
RD, where D ∈ {10,30,50} represents the problem dimensionality. Functions incorporate transformations such
the balance between coherent convergence and randomized motion during the exploitation phase.
● In effective exploitation, agents exhibit coordinated movement toward a basin of attraction, yielding moderately low entropy values (0.20–0.35) that indicate directional consistency without collapse. ● In stagnation, agents exhibit near-identical displacement directions with negligible magnitudes, resulting in very low entropy (< 0.10), signaling premature convergence to a narrow region without further improvement. ● In noisy search, high entropy (> 0.45) emerges due to scattered and uncorrelated directions, indicating lack of convergence focus.
To confirm this interpretation, we conducted controlled simulations in three fake scenarios—pure exploitation, stagnation, and noisy search—and quantified directional entropy over 50 iterations in each instance. The resulting distributions (Fig. 5) demonstrate clear distinction, with median entropy values of about 0.25 for exploitation, 0.05 for stagnation, and 0.55 for noisy search. To avoid confusion in real-world application, our exploitation-aware trigger uses both entropy thresholds and progress checks. An exploitation adjustment is only made after a low-entropy detection event if the global best improves in the last 10 iterations. If it does not, it is considered stagnation and handled accordingly. This dual criterion makes sure that low entropy is accurately understood as effective exploitation instead of search collapse. 3.4 Analytical justification and validation of the entropy–benefit model
The chosen functional form G(H) = 1 − e−k(1−H) is derived from saturation models widely used to capture diminishing returns in systems approaching equilibrium. Here, H denotes normalized local diversity entropy, and k is a sensitivity constant. This form ensures that exploitation benefit vanishes at maximum entropy (pure exploration) and saturates as entropy approaches zero (full convergence). The exponential decay term captures the diminishing marginal benefit of further convergence once a population is already tightly clustered. To validate the model, we compared predicted benefits to observed fitness gains following exploitation triggers across the CEC 2025 30D benchmark. For each trigger event, we recorded Ht and measured the relative improvement over the subsequent 20 iterations. Nonlinear regression was applied to fit k, yielding R2 values in the range 0.82–0.89 across algorithms. These results confirm that the proposed mapping is not only theoretically grounded but also empirically predictive, providing a principled basis for adaptive exploitation scaling. Figure 9 highlights the predictive alignment of the entropy–benefit model G(H) = 1 − e−k(1−H) with observed exploitation gains for PSO, DE, and GWO on CEC 2025 30D benchmarks. The close match between observed points and model prediction confirms theoretical validity and practical predictive power.

4.4 Statistical reliability and error control
To ensure the credibility of our performance comparisons, all statistical analyses were executed with a focus on both statistical power and error rate management. We ran each algorithm–problem–dimension combination 30 times on its own, which is a sample size that is widely acknowledged in the metaheuristics community and is big enough for nonparametric testing. We used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test at α = 0.05 to do pairwise comparisons. We also used the Holm–Bonferroni correction to deal with multiple comparisons and keep the family-wise error rate in check. In an agent's movement path, directional entropy Ht stands in for the amount of information. Low entropy is behavior that is predictable and may not change, while high entropy means disorder or too much exploration. For progress to continue during exploitation, the entropy level should be balanced, meaning it should not be too chaotic or too low. 5.3 Ablation study of behavioral modules
We did ablation tests on the CEC 2025 30D benchmark suite to find out how each behavioral module worked on its own. There were four configurations tested: entropyswitching only (E-only), stagnation detection only (S-only), both modules coupled (E + S), and no behavioral adaptation (classical). The E-only configuration increased performance by an average of 4.7% over the classical baseline. This was mostly because it made it easier to switch between exploration and exploitation phases. The S-only setup showed a 5.1% improvement, and it worked best on multimodal landscapes with plenty of plateaus by restarting the search when progress slowed down. The combined E + S type got a 9.0% mean improvement, which shows that the two modules work well together. Statistical testing (Wilcoxon signedrank, α = 0.05) validated that E + S considerably surpassed both E-only and S-only. These results indicate that although each module is advantageous independently, their amalgamation enhances robustness and performance across various optimization environments. 7 Conclusion and future work
This study proposes a behavioral framework for analyzing and modulating exploitation in representative metaheuristic families. The main contribution lies in making exploitation dynamics measurable and interpretable at the agent level, while showing that behavior-guided interventions can improve refinement on selected difficult benchmark functions. At the same time, the revised results indicate that aggregate benchmark-level advantages should be interpreted cautiously, especially when score differences are numerically small or variability increases. The study therefore offers a diagnostic and methodological contribution more than a claim of universal optimizer superiority. We used this framework to create and test exploitation-aware versions of three popular algorithms: PSO, DE, and GWO. Testing the CEC 2025 benchmark suite showed that these changes to behavior make a big difference in convergence accuracy, robustness, and interpretability. The EA-GWO version did better than all the other baselines in a statistically significant way. This was because it had a mutation mechanism that was triggered by entropy and changed how the agent exploited based on its local state. Our results show
● Guideline for Future Metaheuristic Design: ● Embed interpretable metrics (e.g., entropy, distance decay) directly into algorithm instrumentation from the outset, enabling live detection of inefficiencies or pathological states. ● Use metric-driven triggers for exploitation and diversification to reduce dependence on problem-specific parameter tuning. ● Validate transferability through stress-testing on noisy, dynamic, and black-box problems to ensure robustness beyond standard benchmarks.
● Leverage visualizations in developer workflows to shorten the design-debug cycle and accelerate convergence on effective parameterization.

Add more advanced optimizers like CMA-ES, L-SHADE/SHADE variations, and learning-based metaheuristics as extra baselines and goals for exploitationaware adaptation. This will test both the competitiveness and generality of the behavioral triggers across different algorithm families.
