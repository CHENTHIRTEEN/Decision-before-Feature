ABSTRACT
In recent years, the multi-population mechanism stands out as an effective method to improve the artificial bee colony (ABC) algorithm. However, many existing multi-population ABC variants still exhibit some deficiencies, such as a fixed number of subpopulations and a single type of population partition method. Hence, in this work, a new multi-population ABC variant, called ABC-AMP, is proposed by designing an adaptive multi-population mechanism. For ABC-AMP, to determine the number of subpopulations and the type of population partition method, the fitness landscape analysis technique is first used to identify the problem features. Then, based on the identified features, the number of subpopulations and the most suitable type of population partition methods are determined adaptively. Meanwhile, to preserve the search experience, an improved dual-elite search strategy is crafted in the scout bee phase. To evaluate the performance of ABCAMP, extensive experiments are conducted on two renowned test suites (CEC2013 and CEC2017) and three practical optimization problems. In comparison with four multi-population ABC variants, five other state-of-theart ABC variants and four non-ABC variants, the comparative results demonstrate that ABC-AMP outperforms its competitors, showcasing a competitive performance.
1. Introduction
Swarm intelligence optimization (SIO) algorithm is a kind of global optimization technique, which is based on the inspiration of collective behavior emerged in social animals. The main advantage of this kind of algorithm over traditional optimization methods is that there is no strict requirement on the mathematical properties of the optimization problem, and it can well adapt to the high nonlinearity, strong constraint, and nonconvexity of the optimization problem. As a relatively new paradigm of SIO algorithm, artificial bee colony (ABC) algorithm simulates the intelligent foraging behavior of honey bee colonies in nature [1]. Due to its simple structure yet easy implementation, ABC has drawn wide attention of researchers in the community of SIO algorithm, and has been successfully applied to solve various practical optimization problems, such as job-shop scheduling problems [2], feature selection problems [3], quadratic assignment problems [4], and path planning problems [5]. These multi-population ABC variants have been shown impressive performance, but they still suffer from some common issues in terms of the multi-population mechanism, that the number of subpopulations is often fixed during the entire algorithm procedure, and the population partition method is limited to a single type, such as the random partition method. In fact, for the multi-population mechanism, there are two related important factors: (1) how many subpopulations should be partitioned into, and (2) what kind of partition method should be employed. Both of the two factors have a close relationship with the problem feature. In general, if the problem has a smooth landscape, a small number of subpopulations is preferred, and the good individuals should be first considered for the population partition method. Because this kind of problem is relatively easy to optimize, a few subpopulations can quickly move toward the global optimum, and the good individuals can play the leader role in guiding the search. In contrast, a large number of subpopulations is more suitable for the problem with a rugged landscape, and strong randomness should be involved for the population partition method. The reason is that this kind of problem usually contains many local optimal areas, a large number of subpopulations is helpful to scatter the subpopulations over different subregions of the search space, and the population diversity can be encouraged by randomly partitioning the population. In this work, based on the fitness landscape analysis (FLA) technique [10], an adaptive multi-population ABC variant, called ABCAMP, is proposed. The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows.
• To adaptively adjust the number of subpopulations, a representative FLA technique, namely the fitness-distance-correlation (FDC) method [11,12], is employed, which identifies the problem features into three different types. Thus, according to the problem feature, the number of subpopulations can be adaptively set to a reasonable value. • Considering the number of subpopulations, three different population partition methods are defined, including the random partition method, clustering partition method, and niching partition method. A different number of subpopulations is associated with a different population partition method, so that the flexibility of the multi-population mechanism is enhanced. • Based on the multi-population mechanism, a modified dual-elite search strategy is designed for improving the scout bee phase, aiming to preserve the search experience of the scout bee.
To test the performance of the ABC-AMP, extensive experiments are conducted on two well-known test suites (CEC2013 [13] and CEC2017 [12]) and three practical optimization problems. In the experiments, four multi-population ABC variants, five other ABC variants, and four non-ABC variants are included in the performance comparison, and the ABC-AMP has been shown very competitive performance by surpassing the competitors. In Section 3, details of the ABC-AMP are presented, including the motivation of our approach. The Section 4 discusses the experimental results. Last, in Section 5, this work is summarized and future works are also outlined.

2.2. Relevant works
Given the inherent limitation of the classic ABC, i.e., its strong performance in exploration but weak performance in exploitation, thereby in the community of ABC, an increasing number of improved ABC variants have been proposed. According to the differences of improvement ideas, these relevant ABC variants can be mainly divided into four categories, and each of which is shortly reviewed as follows. (1) New solution search equation-based ABC Regarding the classic solution search equation, where the involved individual is randomly selected from the population, there is a tendency for the search behavior to focus more on exploration than exploitation. Therefore, to solve this issue, a natural idea arises by utilizing the good individuals to design a new solution search equation. For instance, Aslan et al. [6] designed a solution search equation based on the global best (gbest) individual to generate offspring individuals (iqABC). Bajer et al. [15] incorporated an elite individual into the solution search strategy (DRABC), where the top ntop individuals are selected to construct an elite group. Wang et al. [16] proposed a best neighbor-guided ABC (NSABC), in which the best neighbor within the k-neighborhood is utilized as the start point of the solution search equation. To perform a fine search in the onlooker bee phase, Zhou et al. [7] presented a modified solution search equation by extracting the information of multiple elite individuals (MGABC). (2) Multi-population mechanism-based ABC To alleviate the problem of overexploiting good individuals, the multi-population mechanism is an appealing way to enhance the exploitation capability while maintaining population diversity. Recently, Cui et al. [8] presented a dual-population ABC (DPABC) to divide the population into two groups, in which the individuals gradually flow from one group to another in accordance with the division rules. Zhou et al. [9] designed a multi-population ABC (DMABC), in which the number of subpopulations is dynamically adjusted by checking the quality of the global best individual. Moreover, in [17], Zhou et al. employed the multi-population mechanism to partition the entire population into three distinct groups, where each group operates with its unique neighborhood topology (ABC-MNT). A novel ABC variant based on the multi-population mechanism (FLABC) was proposed by Zhou et al. [1], which enhances the search capability by gradually reducing the number of subpopulations during the evolutionary stages. (3) Multiple strategies ensemble-based ABC Unlike a single search strategy, multiple strategies ensemble-based methods have emerged as another mainstream approach in the ABC community. Considering that different strategies may be suitable for different evolution stages, in [18], Gao et al. proposed two solution search equations for the employed bees phase and the onlooker bees phase, respectively (ILABC). Kiran et al. [19] introduced variable search strategy into ABC (ABCVSS), in which five solution search equations are used to find better individuals. Yurtkuran et al. [20] designed a novel ABC based on a multi-strategy ensemble method, called AABC, in which six search strategies are used to generate offspring. Wang et al. [21] presented an improved ABC (BDLDABC), where three distinct search equations are adaptively selected based on the three types of bees. (4) Other search techniques integration-based ABC Apart from the above categories of methods, another significant aspect of the relevant work focuses on the integration of ABC with various other search techniques. For example, as a local search tool, the Hooke-Jeeves pattern search technique was incorporated into ABC by Kang et al. [22] for performing fine search near the best individual (HABC). Moradi et al. [23] combined a chaos-based search technique with ABC (CVABC) to generate new offspring, thereby enhancing the exploitation capability of ABC. Cui et al. [24] introduced the depthfirst search technique into ABC (DFSABC_elite), which assigns more computing resources to the elite solutions. Zhou et al. [12] integrated ABC with the neighborhood search technique (ABC-ANT), aiming to utilize the best neighbor of the current individual for a fine search.
3. Our approach
3.1. Motivation
In the classic ABC, the location of a food source is updated by the solution search equation, in which the involved individual is randomly chosen from the population. This kind of update pattern allows ABC to excel in exploration but hinders its performance in exploitation. Hence, in order to enhance the exploitation capability of ABC, a number of new solution search equations have been devised by leveraging the information of good individuals. Nevertheless, when the good individuals are overexploited, this approach may cause a loss of population diversity and an increased risk of premature convergence. To address this issue, in recent years, the idea of multi-population mechanism has been regarded as an efficient way. In this mechanism, the whole population is divided into several subpopulations, each of which is located in a different search area, thus improving exploitation capacity while maintaining population diversity. To design a multi-population ABC variant, two key factors must be first considered: the number of subpopulations and the method of population partition. Considering the first factor, some multi-population ABC variants still use a fixed number of subpopulations. For example, in [17], Zhou et al. divide the whole population three subpopulations by simultaneously considering the fitness and position information. Once the first factor is determined, the second factor, i.e., the choice of partition method, falls into place subsequently. Currently, almost all the relevant ABC variants only employ a single type of population partition method. For instance, Zhou et al. [1] employed the clustering partition method to divide the entire population into several subpopulations, taking into account the spatial locations of the individuals. Although these multi-population ABC variants have been shown promising performance, they still suffer from some issues due to the fixed number of subpopulations and the single population partition method. In fact, a variable number of subpopulations is more suitable for solving difficult problems or multiple kinds of problems, which provides a means to balance the relationship between exploration and exploitation by adjusting the number of subpopulations. For example, to handle the problem with a smooth landscape which can be considered as a relatively easy problem, a small number of subpopulations is better than a large one, since it is easier for the individuals within different subpopulations to converge together toward the global optimal solution. In contrast, to solve a relatively difficult problem which often has many local optima, a large number of subpopulations is preferred. Similarly, if multiple types of population partition methods can be employed for the multi-population ABC variants, it would be better than just using a single population partition method, as the search capability can be more diverse. Based on the above considerations, in this work, we are motivated to design an adaptive multi-population mechanism, which should include a variable number of subpopulations and multiple types of population partition methods. To this aim, the FDC method, a representative FLA technique, is employed to identify the problem feature, so that the number of subpopulations can be reasonably set and different types of population partition methods can be dynamically chosen. To the best of our knowledge, in the community of ABC, this work may be the first attempt of simultaneously adjusting the number of subpopulations and using multiple types of population partition methods, which could be a meaningful investigation of improving the performance of ABC from the perspective of the multi-population mechanism.

However, at that time, it lacked a formal definition and only provided an intuitive depiction of the evolutionary process. Subsequently, Stadler et al. These three fundamental elements can be utilized to measure or quantify the problem hardness. In the case of continuous optimization problems, for low-dimensional landscapes, it is possible to visually represent the fitness landscape as a surface covering the entire search space. [29], which is used to measure the complexity of a problem by calculating the correlation coefficient, denoted as r ∈ [−1, 1], which represents the relationship between the fitness value and the distance to the global optimum. Following the suggestions of Müller et al. [12], problem features can be broadly interpreted into the following three classes according to the value of r:

Through the analysis of the r classification, it becomes clear that in ideal unimodal problems, there exists a strong positive correlation between fitness and distance. This correlation coefficient can be directly quantified as r = 1 due to the smooth fitness landscapes. Conversely, in un-/anti-correlated problems, the correlation coefficient can be directly quantified as r = −1 due to the rugged fitness landscapes. In addition, for neutral problems, the value of r can be quantified within the range of 0.15 to 0.75 due to the neutral fitness landscape.
Although many different FLA techniques can be used to analyze the fitness landscape, these techniques have diverse features, resulting in different practical applicability. Compared with other FLA techniques, the FDC method exhibits relatively lower computational time complexity without at the expense of accuracy. Given that the primary focus of our work lies in devising a novel adaptive multi-population mechanism rather than developing new FLA techniques, the adoption of a well-established and representative FLA technique like FDC proves adequate. Besides, in some other related works [7,30], the FDC method is also recommended.
3.3. Adaptive multi-population mechanism (AMP)
In this subsection, we provide an in-depth description of the adaptive multi-population mechanism. This mechanism is composed of two main parts, i.e., a variable number of subpopulations and multiple types of population partition methods. These two parts are elaborated on below.
3.3.1. Variable number of subpopulations
In the context of the multi-population mechanism, the decision of how many subpopulations should be partitioned has a critical role in algorithm performance. In general terms, a smaller number of subpopulations facilitates exploitation, whereas a larger number is conducive to exploration. Consequently, in our work, we set the minimum number of subpopulations at a threshold of 2. The purpose of this configuration is to maintain population diversity and prevent overly greedy searches from causing the algorithm to fall into a local optimum. Simultaneously, we cap the maximum number of subpopulations at 10. This upper limit efficiently curbs the consumption of computational resources that can result from an excessively high number of subpopulations. In addition, we employ 6 subpopulations to strike a balance between exploration and exploitation in the algorithm. To establish an appropriate number of subpopulations, we first give a predefined set, represented as S = {2, 6, 10}, and each element in this set S can be viewed as a potential candidate for the number of subpopulations. Following this, we turn to the FDC method to quantify the problem’s features, allowing for the allocation of varying subpopulation numbers to problems with distinct features. To elaborate, if a problem showcases a smooth fitness landscape (r > 0.75), the entire population is divided into 2 subpopulations, which accelerates the convergence speed of the algorithm. Then, for problems characterized by rugged fitness landscapes (r < 0.15), the element 10 is selected from the set as the number of subpopulations to prevent entrapment in local optima. In situations where problems exhibit neutral fitness landscapes (0.15 ≤ r ≤ 0.75), the number of subpopulations is set to 6, achieving an ideal balance between exploitation and exploration. To visually demonstrate the process of how to select variable number of subpopulations, a schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 1. In this diagram, for the sake of convenience, the number of individuals in the whole population is fixed at 20, and each subpopulation comprises a minimum of 2 individuals. Note that, the parameter T, represents the regrouping interval. Too small a T value may disrupt the correct search direction of the population, while too large a value may not allow for timely regrouping when the population stagnates. Therefore, for the proposed AMP mechanism, an appropriate value of T will be ascertained through empirical analysis in the experiments section.

For multi-population ABC variants [1,8,17], the individuals in the subpopulations usually contribute valuable information to guide the search, so the choice of how to group individuals, i.e., the selection of the population partition method, has a significant influence on algorithm performance. Hence, after solving the question of how many subpopulations should be divided, the next question that what kind of population partition methods should be employed requires discussion.

Random partition method [9,31]: Fig. 3 illustrates the process of the random partition method. Specifically, it begins by randomly dividing individuals within the population into multiple subpopulations, ensuring that each subpopulation contains at least SN∕M individuals, where SN represents the population size. In cases where an even division is not possible, the surplus individuals are randomly assigned to different subpopulations. Unlike the niching partition method, this partition pattern greatly bolsters the algorithm’s global search capability, rendering it beneficial for increasing population diversity and tackling problems with rugged fitness landscapes, as it aids the algorithm in jumping out of local minima. Clustering partition method [1,31,32]: In Fig. 4, the clustering partition method is demonstrated, and its primary procedure can be outlined in three steps: (1) Initiating a reference point R, which is randomly generated within the search space. Following this, the individual Xi from the population, which exhibits the closest proximity to R, is selected as the central point of the corresponding subpopulation, given its minimal Euclidean distance to R. (2) To constitute a subpopulation, the top N − 1 individuals from the population that are closest to Xi are selected. (3) Finally, the above N individuals are removed from the population, and the subsequent generation of additional subpopulations is achieved through the cyclic execution of steps 1 and 3. In instances where an equal division is not feasible, the surplus individuals are allocated in the same way as in the niching partition method mentioned above. Contrasted with the random partition method, this partition method, rooted in the individuals’ spatial location, offers a harmonious blend of global and local search capabilities. Hence, the clustering partition method excels in balancing exploration and exploitation, making it particularly well-suited for neutral landscapes. Considering the features of the three kinds of population partition methods described above, it becomes easier to select the most suitable population partition method. Concretely, the niching partition method is chosen for the problem with a smooth fitness landscape. Because the seed individuals (i.e., the good individuals) in the subpopulations can be used to guide the search toward a promising area, providing
a good local search capability. Then, for the problem with a rugged fitness landscape, the random partition method is preferred due to its strong global search capability. For the problem with a neutral fitness landscape, the clustering partition method is employed, because it is beneficial for balancing both exploration and exploitation capabilities.

Hence, to elevate the search performance, there is a need to devise some innovative search strategies, as briefly summarized in subSection 2.2 ‘‘Relevant works’’. However, in our work, the primary focus lies in enhancing algorithm performance by using an adaptive multi-population mechanism, rather than devising entirely new search strategies. Consequently, we opt to directly employ the already well-designed search strategies. 3.5. Fine-tuned dual-elite search strategy
As described in subSection 2.1 ‘‘The classic ABC’’, in the scout bee phase, if a food source is not consecutively renewed for limit times, it is considered as an abandoned food source. Thus, scout bees play a crucial role in the prevention of search stagnation within the population caused by the abandoned food source. They achieve this by generating a new food source to replace abandoned ones, effectively breaking any potential search stagnation. Nevertheless, there exists a drawback associated with the random initialization approach, namely, the possible forfeiture of valuable information embedded in the abandoned food source. In some cases, these abandoned food sources may represent local optimal solutions closer to the global optimal solution of the problem. Hence, there is merit in retaining and utilizing these abandoned food sources during the search process. To do so, in ABC-AMP, we fine-tune the dual-elite search strategy, initially proposed in our prior work, as a substitute for the original random initialization approach. 4. Experimental results and analyses
In this section, to assess the performance of ABC-AMP, a variety of experiments are presented, which consists of following five main parts:
• Sensitivity analysis of control parameters: For the involved parameters, i.e., SN, limit, T , and CR, experiments are carried out to ascertain the parameter value exhibiting the best overall performance of ABC-AMP.
• Effectiveness verification of the proposed modifications: This part focuses on validating the effectiveness of two primary modifications, namely, the adaptive multi-population mechanism and the modified scout bee phase.
• Comparison with multi-population ABC variants: In this part, we conduct comparison experiments between ABC-AMP and four distinct multi-population ABC variants, i.e., DPABC [8], DMABC [9], ABC-MNT [17], FLABC [1], with the aim of verifying the competitive performance of ABC-AMP.
• Comparison with other advanced ABC variants: To further check the competitive performance of our algorithm, in this part, five other advanced ABC variants, i.e., iqABC [6], DRABC [15], MGABC [7], NSABC [16], ABC-ANT [12], are used as competitors. • Application in practical optimization problems: In this part, our algorithm is applied to solve three complex engineering optimization problems, namely, pressure vessel design (PVD) problem [35], speed reducer design (SRD) problem [36], and cantilever beam design (CBD) problem [37].
• Comparison with other non-ABC variants: This part presents a comparative performance of ABC-AMP with other non-ABC variants, such as CMA-ES [38], L-SHADE [39], MSPSO [40], and MACOR [41].
In the experiments, we utilize two extensively adopted test suites: (a) CEC2013, encompassing 28 test problems, and (b) CEC2017, consisting of 29 test problems. The experiments conducted in the first two sections and the final parts are implemented using the CEC2013 test suite with D = 30. As for the third part, our experiments cover both the CEC2013 and CEC2017 test suites, and perform on two distinct test problem dimensions (i.e., D = 30 and D = 50). Note that, both test suites cover a multitude of complex test problems, such as shift, rotation, and composite. For more detailed information on each test problem, please refer to the relevant Refs. 4.1. Sensitivity analysis of control parameters
To ensure the fairness of the experiment, in this paper, identical configurations are applied to all the involved algorithms for the common parameters, with the details as follows, while the rest of the parameters for all involved algorithms follow the original literature.
• Population size: SN = 50. • Problem dimension: D = 30 and 50. • Termination condition of the algorithm: MaxF Es = 10000 ⋅ D.
As for the involved parameters of ABC-AMP, namely, population size (SN), threshold value in the scout bee phase (limit), the regrouping interval (T ), and the crossover rate (CR), this subsection investigates their settings. Moreover, for clarity regarding the distinctions in each parameter value, we record the number of best results (NBR) associated with various parameter values, and the best results are shown in boldface.
4.1.1. Sensitivity analysis of parameter SN
Much like in the classic ABC, the parameter SN of ABC-AMP determines the population size and affects algorithm performance. To elaborate, a larger SN value is preferable for bolstering global exploration, whereas a smaller SN value is more effective for local exploitation. Hence, to determine the optimal population size, we adopt a series of typical SN values for testing, namely SN = 30 [44], SN = 50 [24], SN = 100 [18], and SN = 200 [45]. Note that these SN values are also adopted by other ABC variants. The experiment results are presented in Table 1 and Fig. 5. As indicated by the results in Table 1 and Fig. 5, SN = 50 stands out as the best choice, achieving optimal results on 17 out of 28 test problems, and exhibiting the best overall performance. In contrast, SN = 30, 100 and 200 gets the best results on 7, 8 and 8 test problems, respectively. The rationale behind this phenomenon lies in the fact that an excessively large population size tilts the algorithm heavily towards exploration, whereas an exceedingly small population size inclines it towards exploitation, both of which lead to a degradation in the algorithm performance. Thus, the choice of SN = 50 indicates a population size of moderate scale, facilitating an equilibrium between exploration and exploitation.

4.1.3. Sensitivity analysis of parameter T
In the multi-population mechanism, the regrouping interval T is a key parameter that determines the frequency of regrouping all individuals in the population. In general, a small T value has the capacity to disrupt the population’s proper search direction, while, on the other hand, an overly large T value may lead to search stagnation and plunge the algorithm into local optima. As such, to determine an optimal value of T , this subsection investigates five different values of T , i.e., 1, 10, 20, 50, and 100, and the comparison results are provided in Table 3. 4.2. Effectiveness verification of the proposed modifications
In ABC-AMP, two primary modifications are introduced: (a) the adaptive multi-population mechanism, and (b) the fine-tuned dual-elite search strategy in the scout bee phase. To assess the effectiveness of both modifications, we present experimental results in the following two subsections, namely, subSection 4.2.1 ‘‘Effectiveness verification of the first modification’’ and subSection 4.2.2 ‘‘Effectiveness verification of the second modification’’. These comparative experiments are conducted on the CEC2013 test suite with D = 30.
4.2.1. Effectiveness verification of the first modification
Concerning the first modification, we employ the FDC method to assess problem features, enabling the adaptive adjustment of both the number of subpopulations and the population partition method in accordance with these problem features. As such, this modification consists of two key aspects: firstly, the effectiveness of the FDC method, and secondly, the rationalization of multiple population partition methods. It is imperative to ascertain whether these two aspects indeed exert a positive impact on the algorithm performance. To verify the effectiveness of the FDC method, four distinct ABCAMP variants are crafted as the competitors, each of which is detailed below.
• ABC-1: ABC-AMP omits the FDC method, and divides the population into two subpopulations through the niching partition method. • ABC-2: ABC-AMP omits the FDC method, and divides the population into six subpopulations through the clustering partition method. • ABC-3: ABC-AMP omits the FDC method, and divides the population into ten subpopulations through the random partition method. • ABC-4: ABC-AMP omits FDC method, and both the number of subpopulations and population partition method are randomly selected.

4.3. Comparison with multi-population ABC variants
This subsection focuses on the comparative analysis to check whether ABC-AMP has a competitive advantage over other multipopulation ABC variants. Thus, we compare ABC-AMP with four other multi-population ABC variants in two different dimensions, i.e., D = 30 and D = 50. A summary of these four competing variants is presented below.
• DPABC [8]: ABC based on dual-population framework. • DMABC [9]: ABC with dynamic multi-population scheme. • ABC-MNT [17]: ABC combining neighborhood topologies with multi-population technique. • FLABC [1]: ABC using dynamic subpopulations method.
In DPABC, the population is divided into two subpopulations, and individuals in each subpopulation gradually flow from one to another. For DMABC, a distinctive feature is the dynamic adaptation of subpopulation numbers based on the assessment of the global best individual’s quality. A notable feature of ABC-MNT is the division of the population into three groups, and each group is associated with a unique neighborhood topology. Unlike DMABC, FLABC progressively decreases the number of subpopulations as the evolutionary phase advances,
with the intention of transitioning the search capability gradually from exploration to exploitation. Note that the number of subpopulations in DPABC and ABC-MNT maintain a fixed number, while DMABC and FLABC undergo dynamic changes. Despite this difference, all four variants share a common feature, namely, the utilization of only a single type of population partition method.

For each case, the corresponding comparison results are provided in Tables 8 and 9, respectively. Specifically, on unimodal test problems (F01–F05), ABC-AMP achieves better results on F01–F03 and F05, and only ABC-MNT surpass it on F04. The reason is that ABC-MNT introduces multiple neighborhood topologies and allocates distinct search strategies to individuals within different subpopulations. 4.6. Comparison with other non-ABC variants
This subsection introduces four distinct paradigms of evolutionary algorithms (EAs) for evaluating the performance of ABC-AMP, and the detailed features of those EA variants are provided below:
• CMA-ES [38]: Evolution Strategy (ES) algorithm with covariance matrix adaptation (CMA). • L-SHADE [39]: SHADE with a linear population size reduction method. • MSPSO [40]: Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) with multipopulation framework. • MACOR [41]: Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) with multioperator framework.
The first algorithm, CMA-ES [38], belongs to the ES algorithm variant, and it uses the CMA to determine the new mutation distribution through the previously selected mutation steps. As for L-SHADE [39], it represents an enhanced version of SHADE [39], with the key distinction being its dynamic adjustment of population size throughout the search process, unlike SHADE, which maintains a constant population size. Unlike the first two algorithms, in MSPSO [40], the population is divided into multiple small-sized subpopulations, and then periodically decreases the number of subpopulations during the evolutionary process. The last algorithm, MACOR [41] stands out as a famous ACO variant through its multi-operator framework. This framework can choose the suitable operator by considering the operators’ historical performance and the population’s convergence status during evolutionary process. For objective comparison, all control parameters of the four algorithms adhere to their original literature, with the sole exception being the termination condition set at MaxF Es = 10000 ⋅ D. The experiments are conducted on CEC2013 test suite with D = 30. The results from Wilcoxon’s rank sum test of 30 independent runs, as shown in Table 17, reveal that ABC-AMP exhibits the best overall performance on the test suite. Specifically, on the five unimodal test problems (F01–F05), a comparative analysis reveals that the performance of four competitors either rivals or surpasses that of ABC-AMP. This is evidenced by CMA-ES, L-SHADE, MSPSO, and MACOR show significant superiority than ABC-AMP on 3, 3, 2, and 3 test problems, respectively. The underlying cause may stem from ABC-AMP’s search strategy, wherein excessive randomness in individual selection may undermine its exploitation strength. On the multimodal (F06–F20) and composite (F21–F28) test problems, ABC-AMP achieves better results than the other four algorithms on the majority of the test problems, as they only outperform ABC-AMP on at most five of the 23 test problems. This may be attributed to ABC-AMP’s adaptive multi-population mechanism, which is more effective in solving complex optimization problems. The overall comparison of the algorithms, summarized in Fig. 27, lists their average rankings securing the top position of ABC-AMP, indicating ABC-AMP’s competitive performance compared to other EA paradigms.
5. Conclusions
In this work, we present a novel multi-population ABC variant with an adaptive multi-population mechanism, named ABC-AMP. In contrast to other multi-population ABC variants, the motivation behind ABC-AMP lies in adaptively adjusting the number of subpopulations and population partition methods at the same time. To verify the performance of ABC-AMP, we compare it with nine advanced ABC variants, including four multi-population ABC variants and five other well-established ABC variants, across two test suites (CEC2013 and CEC2017) and three complex engineering optimization problems (PVD, SRD, and CBD). Additionally, to further validate ABC-AMP’s performance, we compare it with four renowned non-ABC variants on the CEC2013 test suite. The comparative results lead to the following conclusions: (1) the population size SN, regrouping period T , control parameter limit, and crossover rate CR should be set to moderate values; (2) the adaptive multi-population mechanism is more effective than other methods, such as the dynamic multipopulation; (3) the fine-tuned dual-elite strategy is more beneficial to enhance the performance than the original dual-elite strategy and random initialization method; (4) the performance of ABC-AMP is highly competitive, outperforming nine advanced ABC variants; (5) ABC-AMP is still able to provide promising performance on the practical problems; (6) In comparison with other non-ABC variants, ABC-AMP still shows a competitive performance. In the future work, we plan to extend the application of the adaptive multi-population mechanism to some other swarm intelligence optimization algorithms.
